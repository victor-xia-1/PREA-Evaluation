Main TWFE takeaway: One additional cumulative year of PREA compliance is associated with lower alleged (−0.73, SE 1.03, p = 0.48) and lower substantiated (−0.12, SE 0.10, p = 0.25) inmate-on-inmate rates per 1,000, conditional on state and year fixed effects and covariates—neither coefficient is statistically significant at conventional levels.

Sexual assault in prisons is an unspeakable crime. When it happens due to the deliberate indifference of correctional au- thorities, it is an affront to fundamental values of fairness and justice as outlined in the Eighth Amendment and Supreme Court Case Farmer v. Brennan. Awareness from the general public, in addition to wider support for a less punitive justice system following mass protests in 2020, reinforces the societal standard clearly stated by nonprofit advocacy organization JDI International, that ”rape is not part of the penalty.” Yet prisons that are unable to be humane in providing shelter or adequate medical care, much less a continuum of reentry programming that supports the reintegration of inmates into the community, are also likely to turn a blind eye to sexual abuse perpetuated within confinement. The need for comprehen- sive federal oversight and guidance on the problem resulted in the passage of the Prison Rape Elimination Act (PREA) in 2003. The Prison Rape Elimination Act establishes standards for confinement aimed at eliminating the problem of sexual as- sault within American prisons, as well as establishing mandatory audits and enforcement mechanisms to ensure state and local compliance. Congress wrote in 2003 that ”experts have conservatively estimated that at least 13 percent of the inmates in the United States have been sexually assaulted in prison,” and that the total number of inmates sexually assaulted in the last 20 years was likely over 1 million (34 USC 30301). In the bill, they also noted that younger incarcerated people were more at risk of being assaulted, that sexual assault in prison inhibits successful reentry, escalates interracial tensions, and that ”members of the public and government officials are largely unaware of the epidemic character of prison rape and the day-to-day horror experienced by victimized inmates.” (34 USC 30301)

Funding to the National PREA Resource Center was cut in April 2025 as part of sweeping DOJ grant cancellations by the Trump Administration (Brennan Center for Justice). The PREA Resource Center also oversaw the PREA audits, the nation’s only oversight tool monitoring compliance with federal rules to end sexual abuse behind bars (Just Detention Inter- national 2025). In addition to empirical analysis, the paper will illustrate why justice reform programs like those funded by PREA, which are intended to respect the dignity and safety of incarcerated people, are important and often lifesaving. The data demonstrate how PREA and associated programs help keep incarcerated adults and children, who are disproportionately racial minorities, safe and maintain oversight and transparency within correctional facilities. Although BJS publishes state-level data on sexual abuse within prisons and jails, we are not aware of any independent empirical analyses on the direct impact of the passage of PREA on reducing the prevalence of sexual assault within American prisons and jails. As funding for PREA resources are threatened by the Trump Administration, we believe an empirical demonstration of the lifesaving effects of PREA is necessary, if possible. Preliminary analysis shows that in many states, reported rates of both allegations and substantiated incidents 1 have increased since the passage of PREA, with a sharp increase at the inclusion of new federal standards. This does not mean that PREA did not work. Since PREA both establishes a standard for improved reporting and data collection and attempts to prevent prison rape itself, the increase in reported incidents after the passage of the act and after release of updated standards in 2012 does not contradict the possibility of real-world improvement after PREA. After all, experts agree that incidence rates were likely underreported due to fear of retaliation before PREA. However, estimating the true benefits of PREA are difficult for this reason.To solve this problem, we apply a method of data analysis known as multivariate regression, which is commonly used in econometrics to estimate the impacts of policies.
